Why preintegrate

From eq. (31) iterated, to three deltas reusable when the starting pose changes.

Lesson 2 integrated a single IMU sample. This lesson explains why we combine a whole interval into reusable deltas, and gives the derivation behind that sentence: one substitution and one multiplication turn Forster's eq. (32) into eq. (33), the preintegrated deltas \(\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ij}\), \(\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}\), \(\Delta\mathbf{p}_{ij}\). It's the core of this course's first goal: deriving them from memory.

0 · Warm-up, from memory

One retrieval from lesson 2, the line this lesson iterates. If you're doing this in the same sitting as lesson 2, do this box again tomorrow too: it only tests memory after a gap.

From memory: write the velocity line of eq. (31), with noise dropped.

$$ \mathbf{v}(t+\Delta t) = \mathbf{v}(t) + \mathbf{g}\,\Delta t + \mathbf{R}(t)\,(\tilde{\mathbf{a}} - \mathbf{b}^{a})\,\Delta t $$
  • \(\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t\), unrotated
  • \(\mathbf{R}(t)\), from the step's start
  • \((\tilde{\mathbf{a}} - \mathbf{b}^{a})\), the bias-corrected specific force

1 · Iterate one step, many times

Between two keyframes \(i\) and \(j\) there are many IMU samples \(k\). Apply eq. (31) to each in turn and the steps stack up:Forster et al., On-Manifold Preintegration…, §VI, eq. (32), plus a matching sum for position. Noise is dropped here, and the bias is taken as constant between keyframes, as Forster assumes in eq. (34).

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{R}_j &= \mathbf{R}_i \prod_{k=i}^{j-1} \mathrm{Exp}\big((\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_k - \mathbf{b}^{g})\,\Delta t\big)\\ \mathbf{v}_j &= \mathbf{v}_i + \mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij} + \sum_{k=i}^{j-1} \mathbf{R}_k\,(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - \mathbf{b}^{a})\,\Delta t \end{aligned} \tag{32} $$

Here \(\Delta t_{ij}\) is the total time from \(i\) to \(j\). Gravity sums to a single \(\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij}\), because it's the same constant at every step.

2 · The problem

Look at \(\mathbf{R}_k\) inside the sum. That's the world orientation at sample \(k\), and it's built on top of \(\mathbf{R}_i\). So every term in eq. (32) depends on the state at keyframe \(i\). Forster says it directly: "a change in the rotation \(\mathbf{R}_i\) implies a change in all future rotations \(\mathbf{R}_k\)", so the whole integration has to be redone whenever the linearization point at \(t_i\) moves.Forster §VI, the paragraph after eq. (32).

Inside an optimizer, the estimate of \(\mathbf{R}_i\) moves every iteration. glass-core's own header puts the cost bluntly: "every one of those hundreds of samples must be re-integrated. Inside an iterative solve that is ruinous."preintegration.hpp, lines 13–18.

3 · The move

Split each world orientation into "keyframe \(i\)" times "the rotation since \(i\)": \(\mathbf{R}_k = \mathbf{R}_i\,\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}\). The rotation since \(i\), \(\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}\), is just the product of gyro steps, so it depends only on measurements. Now pull \(\mathbf{R}_i\) out of the sum and multiply both sides by \(\mathbf{R}_i^{\top}\):

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{v}_j - \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij} &= \mathbf{R}_i \sum_{k=i}^{j-1} \Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}\,(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - \mathbf{b}^{a})\,\Delta t\\ \mathbf{R}_i^{\top}(\mathbf{v}_j - \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij}) &= \sum_{k=i}^{j-1} \Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}\,(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - \mathbf{b}^{a})\,\Delta t \end{aligned} $$

Now look at what sits on each side:

  • Left: only states (\(\mathbf{R}_i, \mathbf{v}_i, \mathbf{v}_j\)) and gravity. It's cheap to re-evaluate every iteration.
  • Right: only IMU measurements and the bias. No \(\mathbf{R}_i\) anywhere, so moving \(\mathbf{R}_i\) can't change it. Integrate it once per interval.

That right-hand side is what gets called \(\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}\). The same move gives \(\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ij}\) and \(\Delta\mathbf{p}_{ij}\).

4 · The three deltas

$$ \begin{aligned} \Delta\mathbf{R}_{ij} &\doteq \mathbf{R}_i^{\top}\mathbf{R}_j = \prod_{k=i}^{j-1} \mathrm{Exp}\big((\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_k - \mathbf{b}^{g})\,\Delta t\big)\\ \Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij} &\doteq \mathbf{R}_i^{\top}(\mathbf{v}_j - \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij})\\ &= \sum_{k=i}^{j-1} \Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}\,(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - \mathbf{b}^{a})\,\Delta t\\ \Delta\mathbf{p}_{ij} &\doteq \mathbf{R}_i^{\top}\big(\mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{v}_i\,\Delta t_{ij} - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij}^{2}\big)\\ &= \sum_{k=i}^{j-1} \Big[\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ik}\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}\,(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - \mathbf{b}^{a})\,\Delta t^{2}\Big] \end{aligned} \tag{33} $$

Each line reads as "state side \(\doteq\) measurement side".Forster §VI, eq. (33). He also warns that, unlike \(\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ij}\), "neither \(\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}\) nor \(\Delta\mathbf{p}_{ij}\) correspond to the true physical change in velocity and position". They're defined to make the right-hand side independent of the state at \(i\) and of gravity. Gravity always ends up on the state side, where \(\mathbf{R}_i\) is already in hand. Inside the sums it would have to be expressed in frame \(i\), which would bring \(\mathbf{R}_i\) straight back.

5 · Where this lives in glass-core

The preintegration header states exactly these three sums:preintegration.hpp, lines 25–31, which go on: "Gravity is deliberately NOT integrated here… Folding it in would re-introduce the dependence on R_i we just worked to remove."

///     dR_ij = prod Exp( (w_k - b_g) dt )
///     dv_ij = sum  dR_ik (a_k - b_a) dt
///     dp_ij = sum  [ dv_ik dt + 1/2 dR_ik (a_k - b_a) dt^2 ]

The loop you saw in lesson 2 is these sums, accumulated one sample at a time. The residual then compares the two sides of eq. (33):nav_residual.hpp, lines 27–43: "Read each as 'what the STATE says happened' minus 'what the IMU says happened'. Zero when they agree."

///     r_dR = Log( dR_hat^T * R_i^T R_j )
///     r_dv = R_i^T (v_j - v_i - g dt)                 - dv_hat
///     r_dp = R_i^T (p_j - p_i - v_i dt - 1/2 g dt^2)  - dp_hat

Notice the _hat. The deltas still depend on the bias, and the bias is a state the solver moves too. How glass-core avoids re-integrating when that changes is lesson 4.

Say it at a whiteboard

"Iterating the IMU from \(i\) to \(j\) puts \(\mathbf{R}_i\) inside every term, so each time the optimizer moves \(\mathbf{R}_i\) you'd re-integrate hundreds of samples. Preintegration writes \(\mathbf{R}_k = \mathbf{R}_i\,\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}\), pulls \(\mathbf{R}_i\) out of the sums, and moves the states and gravity to the other side. What's left, \(\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ij}\), \(\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}\) and \(\Delta\mathbf{p}_{ij}\), depends only on the IMU measurements and the bias. So it's integrated once, and the residual just compares it with the states."

6 · Where \(\mathbf{v}_i\,\Delta t_{ij}\) and \(\tfrac12\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij}^2\) come from

The velocity line was one substitution. The position line takes two more steps, and it is the line people most often get wrong at a whiteboard, so derive it once. Iterate the position line of eq. (31) from \(i\) to \(j\), over \(n\) steps of length \(\Delta t\):

$$ \mathbf{p}_j = \mathbf{p}_i + \sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\Big[\mathbf{v}_k\,\Delta t + \tfrac12\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t^2 + \tfrac12\mathbf{R}_k(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k-\mathbf{b}^a)\,\Delta t^2\Big]. $$

The velocity \(\mathbf{v}_k\) inside the sum is itself built from \(\mathbf{v}_i\). By the velocity line you already derived, with \(\Delta t_{ik} = (k-i)\,\Delta t\) the time elapsed since \(i\),

$$ \mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{v}_i + \mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ik} + \mathbf{R}_i\,\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ik}. $$

Substitute it, and sort every term by what it contains:

  • \(\mathbf{v}_i\) terms: \(\sum_k \mathbf{v}_i\,\Delta t = \mathbf{v}_i\,\Delta t_{ij}\). That is the first mystery term: the distance you would cover coasting at the start velocity.
  • Gravity terms: \(\mathbf{g}\sum_k\big(\Delta t_{ik}\,\Delta t + \tfrac12\Delta t^2\big)\). With \(\Delta t_{ik} = m\,\Delta t\) for \(m = 0,\dots,n-1\), the bracket sums to \(\Delta t^2\big(\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2} + \tfrac{n}{2}\big) = \tfrac12 n^2\Delta t^2 = \tfrac12\Delta t_{ij}^2\). The per-step gravity terms add up to exactly the free-fall distance over the whole interval, which is the second mystery term.
  • Everything else contains \(\mathbf{R}_i\) times a measurement-only quantity: \(\mathbf{R}_i\sum_k\big[\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ik}\,\Delta t + \tfrac12\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k-\mathbf{b}^a)\,\Delta t^2\big]\).

Move the first two groups to the left and multiply by \(\mathbf{R}_i^\top\):

$$ \mathbf{R}_i^\top\big(\mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{v}_i\,\Delta t_{ij} - \tfrac12\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij}^2\big) = \sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\Big[\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ik}\,\Delta t + \tfrac12\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k-\mathbf{b}^a)\,\Delta t^2\Big], $$

which is the third line of eq. (33). Nothing in the derivation was clever: the only idea is “expand everything built on \(\mathbf{v}_i\) and \(\mathbf{R}_i\) and push it to the state side”. If you can reproduce the gravity-sum identity, you can reproduce the whole line.

Try it: check the gravity identity for \(n = 3\).

\(\sum_{m=0}^{2}(m + \tfrac12)\,\Delta t^2 = (0.5 + 1.5 + 2.5)\,\Delta t^2 = 4.5\,\Delta t^2\), and \(\tfrac12(3\Delta t)^2 = 4.5\,\Delta t^2\). Equal, as promised.

7 · Who preintegrates, and who does not need to

Preintegration is not the only way to use an IMU, and knowing the alternatives tells you when it is the right tool.

  • Lupton and Sukkarieh (2012) introduced the idea of summarising the IMU between two poses in the start pose's frame, with rotations parametrised by Euler angles.
  • Forster et al. (2015, 2017), the paper this course follows, moved it onto the rotation manifold, which removed the singularities of Euler angles and gave the clean bias correction and covariance of lessons 4 and 5.
  • Optimisation-based VIO systems, VINS-Mono among them, build their IMU factors this way. VINS-Mono discretises the deltas with a midpoint rule instead of eq. (31), the higher-order choice lesson 2 discussed.
  • Filter-based systems, such as FAST-LIO2's iterated Kalman filter, propagate the state and covariance forward sample by sample, once, and then correct at the LiDAR scan. They never re-linearise a past state, so there is nothing to re-integrate, and they have no need for preintegration at all.

The dividing line is simple. If your estimator revisits past states, as windows and smoothers do, preintegration saves you from re-integrating every time it does. If it never looks back, forward propagation is simpler and just as exact. glass-lio sits in between: it builds a preintegrated factor per scan, and lesson 8 explains why that hybrid is a deliberate stepping stone rather than an accident.

8 · The deltas, computed by hand

Take two IMU samples 5 ms apart from the robot of lesson 2: level, yawing at 0.5 rad/s, reading \(\tilde{\mathbf{a}} = [0.1,\,0,\,9.81]\) with zero bias. Accumulate eq. (33) one sample at a time, updating in the order lesson 2 insisted on: position, then velocity, rotation last.

  • Sample 0. \(\Delta\mathbf{R}_{i,i} = \mathbf{I}\), so the specific force is used as read: \(\Delta\mathbf{v} = [0.0005,\ 0,\ 0.04905]\), \(\Delta\mathbf{p} = \tfrac12\tilde{\mathbf{a}}\Delta t^2\), and the rotation advances by 0.0025 rad about \(z\).
  • Sample 1. The same reading is now rotated by that 0.0025 rad before being added. Its tiny sideways component is the only trace of the turn.

After both samples:

$$ \Delta\mathbf{R}_{ij} = \mathrm{Exp}([0,0,0.005]),\qquad \Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij} = \begin{bmatrix}0.001000\\0.000001\\0.098100\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{m/s},\qquad \Delta\mathbf{p}_{ij} = \begin{bmatrix}0.0000050\\0\\0.0004905\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{m}. $$

Now the payoff. Suppose the solver is unsure which way the robot faced at keyframe \(i\) and tries two very different answers, with \(\mathbf{v}_i = [1.5,\,0,\,0]\) in both. Predicting \(\mathbf{v}_j\) from the state side of eq. (33), \(\mathbf{v}_j = \mathbf{v}_i + \mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij} + \mathbf{R}_i\,\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}\):

trial \(\mathbf{R}_i\)predicted \(\mathbf{v}_j\) (m/s)IMU samples re-integrated
\(\mathbf{I}\), facing \(+x\)[1.501000, 0.000001, 0]none
90° about \(z\), facing \(+y\)[1.499999, 0.001000, 0]none

The same three deltas served both trials. The small forward acceleration lands along \(x\) in one case and along \(y\) in the other, exactly as the rotation says it should, and the IMU samples were never touched again. Also notice the \(z\) row: \(\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}\) has a large \(+0.0981\) component, which the state side's \(\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij}\) cancels precisely. That leads straight to the most common confusion about these deltas.

9 · A stationary sensor with a “velocity change” of 0.98 m/s

Forster warns that \(\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}\) and \(\Delta\mathbf{p}_{ij}\) are not physical changes. The cleanest way to believe him is to preintegrate a sensor that does not move at all. A level IMU at rest reads \([0,0,9.81]\) every sample. Over one 0.1 s LiDAR interval:

$$ \Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij} = \sum_k \tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k\,\Delta t = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0.981\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{m/s}, \qquad \Delta\mathbf{p}_{ij} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0.0491\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{m}. $$

Nearly a metre per second of “velocity change” for a sensor that never moved. Now evaluate the state side with the true states, \(\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{v}_j = 0\), \(\mathbf{R}_i = \mathbf{I}\):

$$ \mathbf{R}_i^\top(\mathbf{v}_j - \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij}) = -\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0.981\end{bmatrix}. $$

The two sides agree exactly and the residual is zero, which is what it should be for a correct state. The deltas are best read as what a falling observer would have seen: the change in velocity the specific force alone would produce, with gravity left for the state side to supply. That is why a residual can be zero while a delta is large, and why you should never log \(\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}\) and read it as how fast the robot went.

A test worth writing

Preintegrate a synthetic stationary sensor for 0.1 s and assert that the residual against a stationary state is zero, not that the deltas are. A test that asserts \(\Delta\mathbf{v}=0\) at rest encodes exactly the misreading this section is about, and it will fail on correct code.

10 · How much it saves, counted

“Ruinous” deserves a number, and the honest number depends on how the deltas are used.

In glass-lio's own tight solve, one IMU factor spans one 0.1 s scan, about 20 samples at 200 Hz. Without preintegration each solver iteration would re-integrate those 20 samples. That is real work but not catastrophic; the bigger wins here are the two things the next lessons build on the deltas: a first-order bias correction (lesson 4) and a covariance computed once (lesson 5), neither of which would be possible if the deltas were rebuilt from scratch at every iteration.

In a window, the saving becomes dramatic. glass-core also serves a sibling project, glass-vio, whose keyframe window keeps 10 keyframes about 0.2 s apart and runs 8 iterations per solve. Each IMU factor therefore spans about 40 samples and stays in the window for 10 solves:

$$ \underbrace{10 \times 8}_{\text{re-linearisations}} \times \underbrace{40}_{\text{samples}} = 3200 \text{ sample integrations per factor, versus } 40 \text{ with preintegration.} $$

An 80-fold saving on every factor, and that is before counting the covariance and the bias Jacobians, which would otherwise be rebuilt just as often. The longer a factor lives inside an optimiser, the more preintegration pays. That is why it is the standard building block of sliding-window and smoothing estimators.

11 · What moving \(\mathbf{R}_i\) still costs: a Jacobian, in closed form

Preintegration does not make \(\mathbf{R}_i\) disappear from the problem; it moves it to the state side, where it appears exactly once per line, in \(\mathbf{R}_i^\top(\cdot)\). That is what makes the residual's derivatives with respect to the starting state cheap closed forms instead of sums over samples. For the velocity line, perturbing \(\mathbf{R}_i\) on the right and using the toolkit's hat rules gives

$$ \frac{\partial\,\mathbf{r}_{\Delta v}}{\partial\,\delta\boldsymbol{\phi}_i} = \big(\mathbf{R}_i^\top(\mathbf{v}_j - \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij})\big)^{\wedge}, \qquad \frac{\partial\,\mathbf{r}_{\Delta v}}{\partial\,\delta\mathbf{v}_i} = -\mathbf{R}_i^\top . $$

Three multiplications, independent of how many samples the interval held.

glass-core computes these as imuJacobianI, and its header records a lesson that is worth knowing before you ever write an estimator. For a long time only the Jacobian with respect to the end state existed, because both front-ends solved for \(\mathbf{x}_j\) with \(\mathbf{x}_i\) held fixed. Holding \(\mathbf{x}_i\) fixed asserts that it is perfectly known. Over a short interval the IMU's information is enormous, so a perfectly known start let the IMU factor outvote every other measurement.nav_residual.hpp, lines 209–233: “Measured on EuRoC V1_01: with this missing, reprojection error grew monotonically to 643 px while 135 landmarks sat in view, unrejected and simply outvoted.” The cure is to treat \(\mathbf{x}_i\) as uncertain, which needs exactly the cheap Jacobian above. Lesson 8 takes this idea to its conclusion: filters, windows and factors differ mainly in how they treat the state at \(i\).

12 · Practice

From memory, no scrolling. The last two questions come from lessons 2 and 1.

Why does eq. (32) have to be re-integrated whenever the optimizer changes \(\mathbf{R}_i\)?

Each acceleration sample is rotated into the world by \(\mathbf{R}_k\), and \(\mathbf{R}_k = \mathbf{R}_i\,\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}\). Move \(\mathbf{R}_i\) and every term in (32) changes.

In \(\mathbf{R}_i^{\top}(\mathbf{v}_j - \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij}) = \sum_k \Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - \mathbf{b}^{a})\,\Delta t\), which side is computed once from the IMU and reused while the solver iterates?

The sum uses only measurements and the bias, so it's integrated once. The state side is a handful of terms, cheap to re-evaluate every iteration. That split is preintegration.

Why does gravity sit on the state side of eq. (33), rather than inside the sums?

Inside the sums, gravity would have to be expressed in frame \(i\), multiplied by \(\mathbf{R}_i^{\top}\): the start orientation, straight back in. On the state side it costs nothing, because \(\mathbf{R}_i\) is already there. glass-core's header says the same.

From lesson 2. In the velocity line of eq. (31), which of these is added without being rotated?

Only \(\mathbf{g}\) is added unrotated. Bias and noise sit inside the bracket \(\mathbf{R}(t)(\tilde{\mathbf{a}} - \mathbf{b}^{a} - \boldsymbol{\eta}^{ad})\), so they're rotated along with the specific force.

From lesson 1. An IMU at rest is rolled so that its body \(y\) axis points straight up. Ignoring bias and noise, what does its accelerometer read, as \((x, y, z)\)?

At rest it reads \(-\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{g}\): +9.81 along whichever body-frame axis points up, here \(y\).

A level IMU sits still for 0.1 s. What is its preintegrated \(\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}\)?

\(\Delta\mathbf{v}\) integrates specific force only: \(9.81 \times 0.1 = 0.981\) up. The state side supplies \(-\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij}\), also \(+0.981\), so the residual is zero. The delta is not a physical velocity change.

The solver tries a new \(\mathbf{R}_i\) rotated 90° from the last one. What happens to \(\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}\)?

\(\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}\) contains no \(\mathbf{R}_i\). The prediction \(\mathbf{v}_j = \mathbf{v}_i + \mathbf{g}\Delta t_{ij} + \mathbf{R}_i\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}\) changes; the delta does not. That is the whole point of preintegration.

Why did an IMU factor with only a Jacobian for \(\mathbf{x}_j\) let the IMU outvote 135 well-observed landmarks?

A constant \(\mathbf{x}_i\) is an assertion of zero uncertainty. The IMU's information over a short interval is large and, combined with a perfect start, it overrules everything else. Treating \(\mathbf{x}_i\) as uncertain needs the Jacobian with respect to it.

From memory: write \(\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}\) both ways, state side \(=\) measurement side, with noise dropped. Then say in one sentence why the right-hand side never needs recomputing when \(\mathbf{R}_i\) moves. Stretch: do the same for \(\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ij}\).

$$ \Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij} = \mathbf{R}_i^{\top}(\mathbf{v}_j - \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij}) = \sum_{k=i}^{j-1} \Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}\,(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - \mathbf{b}^{a})\,\Delta t $$
$$ \Delta\mathbf{R}_{ij} = \mathbf{R}_i^{\top}\mathbf{R}_j = \prod_{k=i}^{j-1} \mathrm{Exp}\big((\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_k - \mathbf{b}^{g})\,\Delta t\big) $$
  • \(\mathbf{R}_i^{\top}\) in front of the state side
  • \(\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t_{ij}\) on the state side, not in the sum
  • \(\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}\), relative to \(i\), inside the sum, not \(\mathbf{R}_k\)
  • The sum uses only measurements and the bias
  • Why: no \(\mathbf{R}_i\) on the right, so moving it can't change the sum
