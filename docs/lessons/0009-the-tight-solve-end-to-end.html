1 · The one equation

A tightly coupled estimator combines motion and geometric evidence in one objective. A prior anchors what the current measurements cannot determine; IMU and LiDAR factors contribute residuals scaled by their uncertainties. A robust loss limits the influence of incorrect point-to-plane correspondences:

$$ \mathcal{X}^{\star}=\arg\min_{\mathcal{X}}\; \|\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{prior}}\|^2_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{prior}}} +\sum_{ij}\|\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{imu},ij}\|^2_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{imu},ij}} +\sum_l\rho\!\left(r_{\mathrm{lidar},l}/\sigma_l\right). $$

The exact state, prior, and number of factors depend on the estimator. This pattern is the LiDAR counterpart of the visual–inertial MAP objective in Forster et al. On-Manifold Preintegration…, §IV, eq. (26); the IMU factor with bias updates is §VI-D, eq. (45).

$$ \begin{aligned} \arg\min_{\mathbf{x}_j,\,\mathbf{g}}\;\; &\sum_{l} \rho\big(r_l/\sigma\big) \;+\; \|\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{I}}\|^{2}_{\Sigma_{\text{eff}}}\\ +\;&\|\mathbf{b}_j - \mathbf{b}_i\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}_b} \;+\; \|\mathbf{g} - \mathbf{g}_{\text{init}}\|^{2}_{\sigma_g^{2}\mathbf{I}} \end{aligned} $$

2 · One scan, in order

Across implementations, the reusable sequence is: choose a common time and frame for the measurements; predict motion from the IMU; associate LiDAR points with map geometry; combine residuals and priors; solve and check the result; then update the map only when the pose is trusted. Each decision needs its own uncertainty model.

Case study: glass-lio's scan update

The following steps show one implementation. Its warm-up length, acceptance threshold, covariance approximation, and gyro-bias feedback gate are design choices, not requirements of tight coupling.

  1. Deskew the points with the gyro, integrated on SO(3) (lesson 2).
  2. Loose for the first 10 scans, to measure the velocity that a static init can't observe (lesson 1).
  3. Preintegrate the IMU over (last scan's end, this scan's end]: the deltas and their covariance (lessons 3, 5).
  4. Grow the bias covariance by its random walk over that interval.
  5. Predict the new state with predictState, putting gravity back in the world (lesson 2).
  6. Iterate the solve: re-associate every point to a plane (lesson 7), stack the four blocks with bias-corrected deltas (lesson 4), solve 18×18, retract.
  7. Accept or coast: too few correspondences, a non-finite solve, or RMSE above the configured 0.5 m, commits the prediction and keeps the scan out of the map.
  8. Carry the posteriors: the bias and navigation blocks of \(H_{\rm total}^{-1}\), the latter becoming the next \(\mathbf{P}_i\) (lesson 8).
  9. Resync deskew's gyro bias, but only if the LiDAR alone constrains rotation (lesson 7's eigen-ratio, on the rotation block).
  10. Insert the deskewed scan into the map, only if the pose was trusted.

This flow spans three functions.processScan() (deskew, downsample, register, insert), registerScan() (the warm-up switch), and registerScanTight(), lines 284–407. Now close your eyes on the list and rebuild it:

  1. Deskew the scan with the gyro
  2. Preintegrate the IMU since the last scan
  3. Predict the new state with the deltas
  4. Stack LiDAR, IMU, bias and gravity rows
  5. Solve the 18×18 system and retract
  6. Accept, or coast on the prediction
  7. Carry the posterior covariances forward
  8. Insert the scan into the map

Steps 4–5 repeat until the increment is small, re-associating points each time, the ICP loop from lesson 6 over a bigger state. Coasting (step 6) skips steps 7 and 8, and the deskew resync sits between them.

The loose path's translation eigen-ratio is not a rejection gate in this tight loop. The rotation-block ratio controls only deskew bias feedback. Carrying a posterior means extracting blocks of the full inverse information, as derived in lesson 8; the total information already includes the priors.

3 · Justify every weight

For any estimator, a residual scale states how much error that measurement model expects. Compare weights only after accounting for units, correlations, and robust losses. Calibrate them from the sensor and the full residual, then test their effect on held-out motion and geometry.

One structural fact ties them together: a LiDAR row has zeros in the velocity, bias and gravity columns (lesson 6). The IMU couples velocity, biases, and gravity to the pose; the bias and gravity priors also constrain their own variables. LiDAR influences velocity indirectly through the joint IMU constraints, including their orientation and bias couplings. Its weight affects the estimate, but does not alone determine velocity error.

Explain the idea: one scan

Put LiDAR and IMU measurements on a consistent time interval and in consistent frames. Use the IMU to predict motion, associate LiDAR points with map geometry, and combine their residuals with priors using justified uncertainty weights. Iterate the state update when the problem is nonlinear, then check whether the measurements constrained the answer. Carry an appropriate uncertainty summary forward and add the scan to the map only when its pose is trusted. The exact state, solver, gates, and feedback rules depend on the estimator.

4 · Who holds what: the information budget of one scan

Section 3 justified each weight on its own. Put them side by side and you can see which block actually determines each part of the state. The table uses glass-lio's configured priors and the preintegration covariance of one 0.1 s scan from lesson 5, before the previous state's inflation, which only lowers the IMU's numbers:

stateLiDARIMU factor (one scan)priorso it is held by…

gyro biaszerothrough rotation10⁴ (rad/s)⁻² initiallyprior, then rotation evidence

gravityzeroweakly, over one scan400 (m/s²)⁻² per axisthe prior, on purpose

Read the right-hand column as a list of vulnerabilities. Velocity has no direct measurement at all: the LiDAR row has zeros there, so velocity is only as good as the IMU's integration, checked through the position it produces. That is why an under-trusted LiDAR let velocity run to 40 m/s. The accelerometer bias starts with information 11 and gains little per scan (lesson 4: only turns reveal it); its random walk adds a variance of just \(10^{-7}\) per scan against a prior variance of 0.09, so the prior dominates for a long time. That is the design working as intended: a weakly observable state should move slowly. Gravity is the one state where the prior is meant to win, because over 0.1 s nothing else can tell a small gravity tilt from a small velocity or bias error. An anchor that followed its own estimate would have given it no restoring force, which is exactly the 500 km failure.

6 · What this solve still leaves out

An honest capstone ends with the edges. glass-lio's tight solve is a careful, measured design, and it is also a deliberately partial one. Each gap below is a real next step, and each connects to a lesson you now have:

  • No window. Only the newest state is solved; the previous one is marginalized out of a single factor (lesson 8). A fixed-lag window would let later scans correct earlier states, and it is where preintegration's savings grow largest (lesson 3).
  • Partial covariance. The carried 15×15 navigation block includes position–bias correlations, but gravity cross-correlations and the complete joint history are not carried forward (lesson 8). A full-state filter would propagate the complete current-state covariance.
  • A fixed gravity anchor. Gravity can move only within its prior around the initial value. That is right for odometry; a system that ran for hours might re-level gravity against a longer history.
  • No loop closure. The map forgets geometry beyond 100 m, so revisiting a place corrects nothing. This is odometry, not SLAM, by choice.
  • Consistency is untested. The covariance has never been checked against ground truth with a statistic like NEES (lesson 8). It is plausible; it is not yet proven honest.

7 · Practice

From memory, no scrolling. The last question comes from lesson 1, where this started.

Why might a tight LiDAR–inertial solver need an external velocity estimate at startup?

A static window can't tell rest from constant velocity, and the test bag starts already moving at ~1.5 m/s. Seed \(\mathbf{v} = 0\) and the IMU factor fights the LiDAR. Loose ICP measures the velocity first; the tight solver starts from it.

What does \(\Sigma_{\text{eff}} = \Sigma_{\text{pre}} + \mathbf{J}_i\mathbf{P}_i\mathbf{J}_i^{\top}\) prevent?

With \(\mathbf{x}_i\) held exact (\(\mathbf{P}_i = 0\)), the IMU's information over 0.1 s is enormous and pins the pose. Inflating by \(\mathbf{x}_i\)'s uncertainty is "the closed-form marginalization of a Gaussian x_i out of this one factor": lesson 8, applied.

Why is the gravity prior anchored to the initial value, not to the previous scan's estimate?

Gravity is nearly unobservable over one 0.1 s scan. An anchor that chases the estimate has no restoring force, so every small pose error is explained by tilting \(\mathbf{g}\): it reached \(|\mathbf{g}|\approx 25\) in 200 scans and threw the pose ~500 km.

In the historical experiment described above, what happened when lidar_sigma was 0.05 instead of 0.02?

A bigger \(\sigma\) trusts the LiDAR less. In that experiment, reducing LiDAR's influence on the joint IMU constraints allowed velocity to drift: ~40 m/s on a bland stretch. A global scale only cancels in the LiDAR-only loose path, never once a second sensor shares \(\mathbf{H}\).

When should an estimator avoid feeding a newly estimated gyro bias back into LiDAR deskew?

Below a rotation eigen-ratio of 0.05, the refined bias is mostly IMU dead-reckoning the LiDAR can't verify. On Corridor02, resyncing anyway corrupted every later scan's geometry and diverged.

From lesson 1. What does the accelerometer in this whole pipeline actually measure?

Specific force, \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}(\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{g})\) plus bias and noise. Every line of this lesson rests on that one equation.

Which block of the tight solve measures velocity directly?

The LiDAR rows have zero velocity columns. Velocity enters only through the IMU factor, which ties it to position and rotation, so it is disciplined indirectly, through the position the LiDAR does measure.

In a log you see \(|\mathbf{v}|\) and \(|\mathbf{b}^a|\) behaving sensibly while \(\mathbf{g}\) drifts from \([0,0,-9.8]\) towards a magnitude of 25. Which term do you inspect first?

A gravity vector that wanders while velocity and bias stay sane is the fingerprint of a gravity prior with no restoring force: anchored to its own moving estimate instead of the initial value.

Early in a run, what mostly determines the accelerometer-bias estimate?

The LiDAR has zero columns for it, and over one scan it is indistinguishable from a small gravity tilt (lesson 4). Its prior information (about 11) dominates until enough turns have separated it from gravity.
