Integrating one IMU step

Where gravity comes back in, and the update-order trap.

Lesson 1 ended on a question. glass-core's integrate() subtracts the biases but never touches gravity, so where does \(\mathbf{g}\) come back? This lesson answers it by building Forster's eq. (31): the update that turns one IMU sample into a change of orientation, velocity and position. If an interviewer says "integrate this IMU sample", this is what you write first.

0 · Warm-up, from memory

Before anything new, one retrieval from last time. Don't open lesson 1.

From memory: write eq. (28), the accelerometer's measurement model.

$$ {}_{B}\tilde{\mathbf{a}} = \mathbf{R}_{WB}^{\top}\big({}_{W}\mathbf{a} - {}_{W}\mathbf{g}\big) + \mathbf{b}^{a} + \boldsymbol{\eta}^{a} $$
  • \(\mathbf{R}_{WB}^{\top}\) in front of the bracket
  • \(\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{g}\) inside it, in \(W\)
  • Bias and noise outside it, in \(B\)

Keep it in view: in section 3 you'll solve this equation for \(\mathbf{a}\).

1 · How the state moves

Forster writes the kinematics of orientation, velocity and position like this:Forster et al., On-Manifold Preintegration…, §V, eq. (29). The hat \((\cdot)^{\wedge}\) turns a 3-vector into its skew-symmetric matrix (§III-A).

$$ \dot{\mathbf{R}}_{WB} = \mathbf{R}_{WB}\;{}_{B}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{WB}^{\wedge}, \qquad {}_{W}\dot{\mathbf{v}} = {}_{W}\mathbf{a}, \qquad {}_{W}\dot{\mathbf{p}} = {}_{W}\mathbf{v} \tag{29} $$

Velocity and position live in the world frame and are plain calculus. The rotation equation is the interesting one: the angular rate is measured in the body frame, so it multiplies \(\mathbf{R}_{WB}\) on the right.

2 · Hold everything constant for one step

An IMU samples every \(\Delta t\). Assume \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) and \(\mathbf{a}\) stay constant across one sample interval, and eq. (29) integrates in closed form:Forster §V, eq. (30).

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{R}(t+\Delta t) &= \mathbf{R}(t)\,\mathrm{Exp}\big(\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\,\Delta t\big)\\ \mathbf{v}(t+\Delta t) &= \mathbf{v}(t) + \mathbf{a}(t)\,\Delta t\\ \mathbf{p}(t+\Delta t) &= \mathbf{p}(t) + \mathbf{v}(t)\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{a}(t)\,\Delta t^{2} \end{aligned} \tag{30} $$

The last two lines are the constant-acceleration formulas from school physics. The first uses \(\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\phi}) = \exp(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\wedge})\), which turns a rotation vector (axis times angle) into a rotation matrix.Forster eq. (6). For the Lie-theory side, see Solà, Deray & Atchuthan, A micro Lie theory for state estimation in robotics. Here \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\,\Delta t\) is the small rotation the body turned through in one step. Multiplying on the right applies it in the body frame, where the gyroscope measured it.

3 · Put the IMU in: gravity comes back

Eq. (30) needs the true \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) and \(\mathbf{a}\), but the IMU only gives measurements. So invert eqs. (27) and (28). The gyroscope is one subtraction. The accelerometer takes three short steps:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{R}^{\top}(\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{g}) &= \tilde{\mathbf{a}} - \mathbf{b}^{a} - \boldsymbol{\eta}^{a}\\ \Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{g} &= \mathbf{R}\,(\tilde{\mathbf{a}} - \mathbf{b}^{a} - \boldsymbol{\eta}^{a})\\ \Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{a} &= \mathbf{R}\,(\tilde{\mathbf{a}} - \mathbf{b}^{a} - \boldsymbol{\eta}^{a}) + \mathbf{g} \end{aligned} $$

The middle step multiplies both sides by \(\mathbf{R}\), using \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top} = \mathbf{I}\). That answers lesson 1's question: gravity comes back here, outside \(\mathbf{R}\). \(\mathbf{R}\) carries the bias-corrected specific force out of the body frame into the world, and gravity was never in the body frame, so there's nothing to rotate.

Substitute both into eq. (30) and you get Forster's discrete-time IMU update:Forster §V, eq. (31). The superscript \(d\) marks discrete-time noise: over one step, \(\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{gd}) = \mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{g})/\Delta t\). glass-core builds exactly that, \(\sigma^2/\Delta t\), in preintegration.cpp, lines 59–66.

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{R}(t+\Delta t) &= \mathbf{R}(t)\,\mathrm{Exp}\big((\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}} - \mathbf{b}^{g} - \boldsymbol{\eta}^{gd})\,\Delta t\big)\\ \mathbf{v}(t+\Delta t) &= \mathbf{v}(t) + \mathbf{g}\,\Delta t + \mathbf{R}(t)\,(\tilde{\mathbf{a}} - \mathbf{b}^{a} - \boldsymbol{\eta}^{ad})\,\Delta t\\ \mathbf{p}(t+\Delta t) &= \mathbf{p}(t) + \mathbf{v}(t)\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t^{2}\\ &\quad + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{R}(t)\,(\tilde{\mathbf{a}} - \mathbf{b}^{a} - \boldsymbol{\eta}^{ad})\,\Delta t^{2} \end{aligned} \tag{31} $$

The measurements, biases and noise are all taken at time \(t\): Forster writes the \((t)\) out, and it's dropped here to fit the page. Read it term by term: gravity appears as \(\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t\) and \(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t^{2}\), unrotated, because it's a constant world-frame acceleration. The IMU's contribution is rotated by \(\mathbf{R}(t)\).Holding \(\mathbf{R}(t)\) fixed across the step is not exact while the IMU rotates. Forster notes that a high-rate IMU keeps the error small, and that slower IMUs may call for higher-order integration (§V).

4 · The update-order trap

Look at what sits on the right-hand side of eq. (31): everything is at time \(t\), the start of the step. Velocity and position both use \(\mathbf{R}(t)\), not the rotation you've just computed, and position uses \(\mathbf{v}(t)\), not the new velocity. In code, that forces the order: position first, then velocity, rotation last.

glass-core does exactly this.preintegration.cpp, lines 85–90. Its comment: "Update dR first and you have integrated position with the wrong orientation." And on the last line: "compose ON the manifold, on the RIGHT (body-frame increment)". Here \(\mathbf{R}(t)\) becomes dR_, the orientation relative to the start of the preintegration window, and \(\mathbf{g}\) is left out:

dp_ += dv_ * dt + 0.5 * dR_mat * a * dt * dt;
dv_ += dR_mat * a * dt;
dR_ = dR_ * dRk;

5 · Where glass-core puts gravity back

The deltas are gravity-free, so gravity has to return somewhere. It comes back once, over the whole interval, in predictState():nav_residual.hpp, lines 152–162.

xj.R = xi.R * pre.dR();
xj.v = xi.v + gravity * dt + xi.R * pre.dv();
xj.p = xi.p + xi.v * dt + 0.5 * gravity * dt * dt + xi.R * pre.dp();

It's the same shape as eq. (31): gravity unrotated in the world frame, and the IMU part rotated by the orientation at the start, xi.R. The code's comment says why gravity was kept out of the deltas: "so the deltas would not depend on xi". Why that matters is lesson 3.

Say it at a whiteboard

"For one IMU step, orientation is right-multiplied by the Exp of the bias-corrected gyro times \(\Delta t\), because the rate is in the body frame. For velocity and position, I rotate the bias-corrected specific force into the world with the orientation at the start of the step, then add gravity back unrotated, because gravity lives in the world frame. Everything on the right-hand side is at the start of the step, so in code I update position, then velocity, and rotation last."

6 · One step, with real numbers

Symbols hide mistakes that numbers expose, so push one sample through eq. (31) by hand. Take glass-lio's test bag at the moment it starts: the robot cruising forward at 1.5 m/s, level, turning gently. The IMU runs at 200 Hz, so \(\Delta t = 0.005\) s. Set the biases to zero for now (lesson 4 puts them back) and use

$$ \mathbf{R}(t)=\mathbf{I},\quad \mathbf{v}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}1.5\\0\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad \tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0.5\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{rad/s},\quad \tilde{\mathbf{a}}=\begin{bmatrix}0.1\\0\\9.81\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{m/s^2},\quad \mathbf{g}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\-9.81\end{bmatrix}. $$

Rotation. The rotation vector for the step is \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\,\Delta t = [0,0,0.0025]\): a quarter of a milliradian about \(z\), 0.14°. \(\mathbf{R}(t+\Delta t) = \mathrm{Exp}([0,0,0.0025])\).

Velocity. Three contributions, added:

$$ \mathbf{v}(t+\Delta t) = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1.5\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{v}(t)} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\-0.04905\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0.0005\\0\\0.04905\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{R}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}\,\Delta t} = \begin{bmatrix}1.5005\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}. $$

Look at the \(z\) components. The accelerometer's +9.81 and gravity's −9.81 cancel exactly, and the robot does not fall through the floor. This cancellation is the single best sanity check on an IMU integrator: feed it a stationary sensor and nothing may move. If your integrator drifts at rest, you have the sign of lesson 1 wrong, or the units, or you rotated gravity.

Position.

$$ \Delta\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{v}(t)\,\Delta t + \tfrac12\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t^2 + \tfrac12\mathbf{R}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}\,\Delta t^2 = \begin{bmatrix}0.0075\\0\\0\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\-0.000123\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}0.00000125\\0\\0.000123\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0.00750125\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{m}. $$

Seven and a half millimetres, almost all of it from the velocity the robot already had. The acceleration contributes about a micrometre. That ratio is typical: over a single IMU step, position is dominated by velocity, and velocity is dominated by gravity and specific force nearly cancelling. Small errors in that near-cancellation are what grow into drift, which is why the next two sections care about details that look negligible here.

Try it: redo the velocity step with the sensor tilted 7.33° about \(y\) but \(\mathbf{R}(t)\) still set to \(\mathbf{I}\).

At rest a tilted sensor reads \(\tilde{\mathbf{a}} \approx [1.25,0,9.73]\) (lesson 1). With \(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\), the vertical terms leave \(-0.08\times0.005\) and the horizontal reading is taken as a forward acceleration of 1.25 m/s², which after one second is 1.25 m/s of velocity the robot never had. Getting \(\mathbf{R}(t)\) right is not optional; this is why initialisation levels the world first.

7 · How good is “hold everything constant”?

Eq. (30) assumes \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) and \(\mathbf{a}\) are constant across the step, and eq. (31) rotates the specific force with the orientation at the start of the step. While the sensor turns, the true orientation drifts away from \(\mathbf{R}(t)\) during the step, by up to \(|\boldsymbol{\omega}|\,\Delta t\), about half of that on average. So the specific force is applied in a direction that is off by roughly \(|\boldsymbol{\omega}|\Delta t/2\), and for a specific force of about \(g\) that is a sideways error of about \(g\,|\boldsymbol{\omega}|\,\Delta t/2\), acting like a small spurious acceleration:

IMU rateturn rateequivalent acceleration errorcompared with accel bias σ = 0.3 m/s²
200 Hz1 rad/s0.025 m/s²a tenth of it: negligible
200 Hz3 rad/s0.074 m/s²still small
50 Hz1 rad/s0.098 m/s²a third of it
50 Hz3 rad/s0.29 m/s²as large as the bias itself

This is the quantitative version of Forster's remark that a high-rate IMU keeps the error small and slower IMUs may need higher-order integration. At the Mid-360's 200 Hz, holding everything constant is comfortably inside the noise and bias budget, so the simplest scheme is the right one. On a 50 Hz IMU mounted on something that spins, the same scheme would feed the bias estimator an error as large as the bias it is trying to find, and a midpoint scheme (average the start and end orientations) would be worth its complexity.

Checked against an exact answer

The table is an estimate. You can check it on a case with a closed-form answer: a level platform yawing at a constant 3 rad/s with a constant 1 m/s² specific force along its own forward axis. In the world that force rotates, and one second of it integrates exactly to \(\mathbf{v}(1) = \big[\sin 3/3,\ (1-\cos 3)/3\big] \approx [0.0470,\ 0.6633]\) m/s. Integrating the samples instead:

IMU ratestart-of-step \(\mathbf{R}\), eq. (31)mid-step \(\mathbf{R}\) (midpoint)
50 Hz2.0×10⁻² m/s1.0×10⁻⁴ m/s
200 Hz5.0×10⁻³ m/s6.2×10⁻⁶ m/s

Two things to read off it. First, quadrupling the rate cuts the eq. (31) error by 4 and the midpoint error by 16: the first is a first-order method, the second second-order, exactly as the theory says. Second, the midpoint scheme at 50 Hz beats eq. (31) at 200 Hz by a factor of fifty. When the IMU rate is fixed by hardware, the integrator is the knob you actually have.

Two integrators in one codebase

glass-lio integrates the gyro in two places, and they deliberately differ. The deskew integrator, which only needs rotation within one scan, averages consecutive gyro samples (the trapezoidal rule, second-order accurate) and composes on the right.gyr_int.cpp, lines 54–55. The preintegration that feeds the tight solve holds each sample constant over its interval, a zero-order hold, exactly like eq. (31).lio_estimator.cpp, line 267: “zero-order hold over the interval”. The first is cheap to improve because it only touches rotation. The second has to stay matched to the Jacobians and the covariance built on eq. (31) in lessons 4 and 5, so changing its integration scheme would mean changing all of those too. Consistency between the integrator and its derivatives is worth more than a better integrator on its own.

8 · Which samples, over which interval?

Eq. (31) tells you how to apply one sample. The question that actually caused a bug in glass-lio is which samples to apply, and over exactly how long.

A LiDAR scan lasts 0.10 s. To deskew it, the synchroniser deliberately hands the estimator more IMU than that: one sample before the scan starts, so the gyro can be interpolated at the first point, and coverage past the end, about 0.12 s in all. That extra coverage is right for deskewing. It is wrong for preintegration. The tight solve relates the pose at the end of one scan to the pose at the end of the next, which are 0.10 s apart. Integrating all 0.12 s of IMU tells the solver “you moved this much” over an interval 20% longer than the one the poses span.

At the robot's 1.5 m/s, that is 0.18 m of claimed motion against 0.15 m of real motion, every scan, always in the direction of travel. The estimator accelerated away, and because the error scaled with speed and compounded, it looked exactly like a gravity problem. The fix was to clip every sample's interval to the window between the previous scan's end and this scan's end, including partial intervals at both edges:lio_estimator.cpp, lines 237–276: the comment block and the clipping loop.

const double lo = std::max(ta, t_begin);
const double hi = std::min(tb, t_end);
const double dt = hi - lo;
if (dt <= 0.0 || dt > kMaxImuGapSec) {
  continue;   // outside the window, duplicate, out of order, or a hole
}

Notice the second condition. A gap longer than 0.1 s between two samples is a hole in the stream, not a sample interval. Holding one reading constant across a hole would invent tenths of a second of motion, so the loop skips it and lets the preintegration's covariance, lesson 5, reflect that less was measured.

The general lesson

Every integrated quantity has an interval, and the interval is part of the measurement. When two parts of a pipeline need different windows over the same stream (here, deskew and preintegration), give each its own window explicitly. Sharing one because it is convenient is how a 20% error hides in plain sight.

9 · Why the IMU cannot hold position alone

Eq. (31) is exact about the sensor you give it and blind to the errors you do not. Iterate it and each error source grows at its own characteristic rate. The table uses glass-lio's configured accelerometer noise density, a residual accelerometer bias of 0.05 m/s² (the size the tight solve settles near on the test bag), and a residual gyro bias of 0.001 rad/s:

timeaccel bias: \(\tfrac12 b_a t^2\)accel noise: \(\sigma_a t^{3/2}/\sqrt3\)gyro bias tilt: \(g\,b_g t^3/6\)
0.1 s (one scan)0.25 mm0.37 mm0.002 mm
1 s2.5 cm1.2 cm1.6 mm
10 s2.5 m0.37 m1.6 m
60 s90 m5.4 m353 m

The exponents are the story. A constant accelerometer bias is integrated twice, so it grows with \(t^2\). White noise is integrated twice too, but it partly averages out, giving \(t^{3/2}\). A gyro bias is integrated once into a tilt, the tilt leaks gravity into the horizontal (lesson 1's tilted-sensor case), and that is integrated twice more: \(t^3\). It is the smallest term at one second and by far the largest at one minute.

Read the first row again. Over the 0.1 s between two LiDAR scans, every one of these errors is below half a millimetre, far below the centimetres a LiDAR can resolve. Over a minute they are tens to hundreds of metres. That asymmetry is the entire design of a LiDAR-inertial odometry: the IMU is excellent over short intervals and useless over long ones, so let it bridge the gap between two scans and let the LiDAR correct it at every scan. Lesson 3 turns “bridge the gap between two scans” into a single reusable measurement.

Try it: why does the bias term use \(t^2/2\) while the gyro term uses \(t^3/6\)?

A constant accelerometer bias is an acceleration: integrate twice, \(\iint b_a = b_a t^2/2\). A constant gyro bias gives a tilt that grows, \(\theta = b_g t\), and so a horizontal acceleration \(g\,b_g t\) that grows linearly; integrating that twice gives \(g\,b_g t^3/6\). Each integration adds one power of \(t\).

10 · Practice

From memory, no scrolling. The last question comes from lesson 1: mixing old and new material is what makes it stick.

Solve eq. (28) for the true world acceleration \(\mathbf{a}\), ignoring noise. Which is right?

Subtract the bias, multiply by \(\mathbf{R}\) to leave the body frame (since \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top} = \mathbf{I}\)), then add \(\mathbf{g}\) to both sides. \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\) would take you into the body frame, the wrong direction.

In the velocity line of eq. (31), why isn't \(\mathbf{g}\) multiplied by \(\mathbf{R}\)?

\(\mathbf{R}\)'s job is to carry the body-frame specific force into the world. Gravity is a world-frame vector and never lived in the body frame, so there's nothing to rotate.

In eq. (31), and in glass-core's integrate(), which orientation multiplies the specific force in the velocity and position updates?

Everything on the right-hand side of (31) is at time \(t\). That's why the code updates position, then velocity, then rotation last. Averaging start and end (a midpoint scheme) is a legitimate higher-order method, but it isn't what (31) or integrate() do.

Why does the rotation update right-multiply, \(\mathbf{R}(t)\,\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\omega}\Delta t)\), rather than \(\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\omega}\Delta t)\,\mathbf{R}(t)\)?

In eq. (29) the rate multiplies \(\mathbf{R}\) on the right because \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) is measured in \(B\). Left-multiplying would apply the step in the world frame instead: in general a different rotation, since \(\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathrm{Exp}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi})\) (Forster eq. 11).

From lesson 1. A level IMU accelerates straight up at 1 g. What does its \(z\) axis read?

\(\mathbf{a} = [0,0,+9.81]\) and \(\mathbf{g} = [0,0,-9.81]\), so \(\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{g} = [0,0,+19.62]\). The sensor feels 2 g: the floor pushing twice as hard as at rest.

A level, stationary IMU with perfectly known biases is pushed through one step of eq. (31). What happens to the velocity?

The sensor reads +9.81 up, rotated by \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\), and gravity adds −9.81: they cancel. This is the first test to run on any integrator. A drift at rest means a sign, unit or frame mistake.

The IMU group for a scan covers about 0.12 s. Why does glass-lio clip the preintegration to a shorter interval?

The IMU factor must span exactly the interval between the two poses it connects: the previous scan's end to this scan's end. Integrating 0.12 s against a 0.10 s pose change claims 20% too much motion, every scan.

A 50 Hz IMU on a platform spinning at 3 rad/s is integrated with eq. (31). The error from holding \(\mathbf{R}(t)\) fixed across each step is closest to…

About \(g\,|\boldsymbol{\omega}|\,\Delta t/2 = 9.81 \times 3 \times 0.02 / 2 \approx 0.29\) m/s². At 200 Hz and 1 rad/s it would be 0.025. The approximation is good because the IMU is fast, not because it is exact.

From memory: write the three lines of eq. (31), bias-corrected, with the noise dropped. Then say the whiteboard sentence out loud.

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{R}(t+\Delta t) &= \mathbf{R}(t)\,\mathrm{Exp}\big((\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}} - \mathbf{b}^{g})\,\Delta t\big)\\ \mathbf{v}(t+\Delta t) &= \mathbf{v}(t) + \mathbf{g}\,\Delta t + \mathbf{R}(t)(\tilde{\mathbf{a}} - \mathbf{b}^{a})\,\Delta t\\ \mathbf{p}(t+\Delta t) &= \mathbf{p}(t) + \mathbf{v}(t)\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t^{2}\\ &\quad + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{R}(t)(\tilde{\mathbf{a}} - \mathbf{b}^{a})\,\Delta t^{2} \end{aligned} $$
  • \(\mathrm{Exp}\) of the bias-corrected gyro times \(\Delta t\), on the right
  • \(\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t\) and \(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t^{2}\), never rotated
  • \(\mathbf{R}(t)\), the step's start, in front of \((\tilde{\mathbf{a}} - \mathbf{b}^{a})\) in both
  • The old velocity \(\mathbf{v}(t)\,\Delta t\) in the position line
  • \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) on both \(\Delta t^{2}\) terms
