The noise preintegration carries

One covariance, propagated a sample at a time, and why it decides who wins.

The deltas tell the solver what the IMU says happened. The solver also needs to know how much to believe it: that's the 9×9 covariance \(\Sigma_{ij}\) of the deltas. Its inverse is the weight on the IMU residual, and in glass-lio it's what gets set against the LiDAR. This lesson is how that covariance is built, one sample at a time. It's the second half of the course's uncertainty-propagation goal, and the answer to an interview question you should expect: how do you weigh the IMU against another sensor?

0 · Warm-up, from memory

The position line of eq. (31), from lesson 2. The noise update in this lesson has exactly its shape.

From memory: write the position line of eq. (31), with noise dropped.

$$ \mathbf{p}(t+\Delta t) = \mathbf{p}(t) + \mathbf{v}(t)\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{g}\,\Delta t^{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{R}(t)(\tilde{\mathbf{a}} - \mathbf{b}^{a})\,\Delta t^{2} $$
  • The old velocity, times \(\Delta t\)
  • \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) on both \(\Delta t^{2}\) terms
  • \(\mathbf{R}(t)\) in front of the bias-corrected specific force

1 · Why a covariance at all

Least squares weights each residual by its inverse covariance. Forster calls modelling it "of paramount importance… the inverse noise covariance is used to weight the terms in the optimization."Forster et al., On-Manifold Preintegration…, §VI-B. glass-lio does exactly that. The IMU factor's weight is the inverse of the preintegration covariance, while each LiDAR point is whitened by lidar_sigma:tight_registration.cpp, lines 118–129. glass-lio also adds the previous state's own uncertainty, \(\mathbf{J}_i\mathbf{P}_i\mathbf{J}_i^{\top}\), which marginalizes an independent Gaussian perturbation of x_i from this one linearized factor. The LiDAR side is at lines 64–70.

const Eigen::Matrix<double, 9, 9> sigma_eff =
  pre.covariance() + Ji * xi_cov * Ji.transpose();
Eigen::Matrix<double, 9, 9> imu_information =
  sigma_eff.inverse() * params.imu_prior_weight;

This matrix helps set the balance with LiDAR. The covariance inflation is exact for that local linear Gaussian model with independent noise; it is not the marginalization of every historical factor or a proof of consistency of the whole estimator.

2 · The noise is linear, so propagate it linearly

Forster writes each delta as "the (to-be-estimated) state 'plus' a random noise", stacked as \(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\Delta} = [\delta\boldsymbol{\phi};\ \delta\mathbf{v};\ \delta\mathbf{p}]\), and shows that, to first order, this is "a linear function of the IMU measurement noise". That makes the covariance "a simple linear propagation". Better still, it can be done iteratively: "as a new IMU measurement arrive[s] we only update \(\Sigma_{ij}\), rather than recomputing it from scratch."Forster §VI-A (eq. 38) and §VI-B (eqs. 39–43). The iterative form is Appendix IX-A.

3 · One sample at a time

With \(\mathbf{a}_k \doteq \tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - \mathbf{b}^{a}\) and the raw IMU noise \(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{d}_k = [\boldsymbol{\eta}^{gd}_k;\ \boldsymbol{\eta}^{ad}_k]\), each sample updates the noise, and so the covariance, like this:Forster Appendix IX-A, eqs. (59)–(63), written there from \(j-1\) to \(j\); here from \(k\) to \(k+1\). \(\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{k,k+1}\) is this step's rotation, and \(\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}\) the rotation accumulated before it.

$$ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\eta}^{\Delta}_{i,k+1} &= \mathbf{A}_k\,\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\Delta}_{ik} + \mathbf{B}_k\,\boldsymbol{\eta}^{d}_k\\ \Sigma_{i,k+1} &= \mathbf{A}_k\,\Sigma_{ik}\,\mathbf{A}_k^{\top} + \mathbf{B}_k\,\Sigma_{\eta}\,\mathbf{B}_k^{\top}, \qquad \Sigma_{ii} = \mathbf{0} \end{aligned} \tag{63} $$
$$ \mathbf{A}_k = \begin{bmatrix} \Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{k,k+1}^{\top} & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0}\\ -\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}\,\mathbf{a}_k^{\wedge}\,\Delta t & \mathbf{I} & \mathbf{0}\\ -\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}\,\mathbf{a}_k^{\wedge}\,\Delta t^{2} & \mathbf{I}\,\Delta t & \mathbf{I} \end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{B}_k = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{J}_r^{k}\,\Delta t & \mathbf{0}\\ \mathbf{0} & \Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}\,\Delta t\\ \mathbf{0} & \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}\,\Delta t^{2} \end{bmatrix} $$

It starts at zero because an empty interval has measured nothing yet. The raw noise covariance \(\Sigma_{\eta}\) comes from the datasheet densities, turned into discrete-time noise exactly as in lesson 2: \(\sigma^{2}/\Delta t\).

Here \(\mathbf{J}_r^k=\mathbf{J}_r((\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_k-\mathbf{b}^g)\Delta t)\) is the right Jacobian of SO(3): it converts an additive error in a rotation vector into a small right-multiplied rotation. It tends to \(\mathbf{I}\) near zero.

The covariance recursion assumes the new measurement noise is independent of the accumulated error. The densities use the convention \(Q_d=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_g^2\mathbf I,\sigma_a^2\mathbf I)/\Delta t\). This 9×9 matrix conditions on the chosen integration biases; it does not include their uncertainty or random walk.

4 · Read it like eq. (31)

Don't memorise A and B as matrices. Read them row by row, and they're the warm-up's equation, linearised:

  • Position row: the error picks up the old velocity error times \(\Delta t\) (the \(\mathbf{I}\,\Delta t\)), and accelerometer noise enters with \(\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}\,\Delta t^{2}\), exactly where the specific force does in (31).
  • Velocity row: accelerometer noise enters with \(\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}\,\Delta t\), again mirroring (31).
  • The first column: an orientation error tilts the specific force slightly the wrong way. To first order that's \(-\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}\,\mathbf{a}_k^{\wedge}\,\delta\boldsymbol{\phi}\), fed into velocity and position.
  • Rotation row: the old error, re-expressed in the new frame, plus fresh gyro noise.

glass-core builds exactly these blocks, term for term:preintegration.cpp, lines 40–68. It runs before the state update in the same loop: "it linearises about the CURRENT state, so it needs dR before we advance it." That's the update-order trap from lesson 2, again.

A.block<3, 3>(0, 0) = dRk.matrix().transpose();
A.block<3, 3>(3, 0) = -dR_mat * a_hat * dt;
A.block<3, 3>(6, 0) = -0.5 * dR_mat * a_hat * dt * dt;
A.block<3, 3>(6, 3) = Eigen::Matrix3d::Identity() * dt;

B.block<3, 3>(0, 0) = Jr * dt;
B.block<3, 3>(3, 3) = dR_mat * dt;
B.block<3, 3>(6, 3) = 0.5 * dR_mat * dt * dt;

cov_ = A * cov_ * A.transpose() + B * Q * B.transpose();

5 · Accumulating noise is not a monotonicity theorem

Each sample adds a positive semidefinite term \(\mathbf B\Sigma_\eta\mathbf B^\top\) to the propagated covariance \(\mathbf A\Sigma\mathbf A^\top\). This does not imply \(\Sigma_{k+1}-\Sigma_k\succeq0\): propagation rotates and couples errors, so individual directional variances need not increase at every step.

For example, let \(\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)\), let \(A\) rotate by 90°, and add \(Q=0.1I\). Then \(A\Sigma A^\top+Q=\operatorname{diag}(1.1,4.1)\). The first variance fell despite fresh noise. Longer inertial dead reckoning generally accumulates error, but that practical trend is not an ordering of every covariance matrix or its inverse.

In the simpler scalar model \(v_{k+1}=v_k+\eta_k\Delta t\), with independent \(\operatorname{Var}(\eta_k)=\sigma_a^2/\Delta t\), the variance is exactly \(\sigma_a^2T\). Doubling \(T\) doubles that variance and halves its information. State the model before drawing this conclusion.

Noise densities are physical parameters to measure or obtain from a suitable sensor specification. Validate their units, bandwidth assumptions, and the resulting residual statistics. Datasheet range noise alone is not the full point-to-plane residual uncertainty: plane fitting, deskew errors, and correlated returns also matter. The historical glass-lio settings are calibration choices for those experiments, not universal constants.

Say it at a whiteboard

"Preintegration's noise is a linear function of the IMU's white noise, so I propagate its 9×9 covariance over \([\delta\boldsymbol{\phi}, \delta\mathbf{v}, \delta\mathbf{p}]\) one sample at a time: \(\Sigma \leftarrow \mathbf{A}\Sigma\mathbf{A}^{\top} + \mathbf{B}\Sigma_{\eta}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\), starting from zero, with the datasheet densities turned into discrete covariances \(\sigma^{2}/\Delta t\). A and B are eq. (31) linearised, plus one coupling: an orientation error tilts the specific force. The inverse of that covariance weights the IMU residual. Dead reckoning generally accumulates uncertainty, but propagation does not guarantee monotonic growth in every direction."

6 · One scan's covariance, computed

Run the recursion of section 3 over one real glass-lio interval: 0.1 s at 200 Hz, 20 samples, with the Mid-360's configured densities \(\sigma_g = 1.7\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad/s/\sqrt{Hz}}\) and \(\sigma_a = 2\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m/s^2/\sqrt{Hz}}\), for a level sensor reading \(\mathbf{a}_k = [0,0,9.81]\). The standard deviations at the end of the scan are the square roots of the diagonal of \(\Sigma_{ij}\):

blockstandard deviation per axisin words
\(\delta\boldsymbol{\phi}\)5.4×10⁻⁴ rad0.03°: the gyro alone knows the scan's rotation extremely well
\(\delta\mathbf{v}\)6.3×10⁻³ m/sabout 6 mm/s
\(\delta\mathbf{p}\)3.7×10⁻⁴ ma third of a millimetre

Each number matches the simple formulas you could derive by hand for a non-rotating sensor: \(\sigma_g\sqrt{T}\) for rotation, \(\sigma_a\sqrt{T}\) for velocity, and \(\sigma_a T^{3/2}/\sqrt3\) for position. The one coupling the recursion adds, orientation error tilting the specific force, is there but small over a tenth of a second: the correlation between a velocity axis and the perpendicular rotation axis is only 0.04. Run the same recursion for a second, or for a minute, and that coupling becomes the dominant term, which is lesson 2's \(t^3\) drift seen from the covariance side.

A test the recursion must pass: the sampling rate must not matter

Run the same 0.1 s interval at 400 Hz instead of 200 Hz. Each step's discrete noise \(\sigma^2/\Delta t\) doubles, and there are twice as many steps, each weighted by a smaller \(\Delta t\) or \(\Delta t^2\) in \(\mathbf{B}_k\). The result:

rateσ rotationσ velocityσ position
200 Hz5.376×10⁻⁴6.331×10⁻³3.652×10⁻⁴
400 Hz5.376×10⁻⁴6.332×10⁻³3.653×10⁻⁴

The same to three or four digits. That is not a coincidence; it is what a noise density means. The physical uncertainty after 0.1 s cannot depend on how often you happened to sample it. This makes an excellent unit test, and it catches the single most common mistake in writing this code: multiplying by \(\Delta t\) instead of dividing. With \(\sigma^2\Delta t\), doubling the rate would halve the variance, and a faster IMU would appear to make the estimator more certain for free.

7 · “Who wins”: the arithmetic behind a real calibration fix

The covariance's inverse is the IMU's information. Per position axis, one scan's IMU factor carries about \(1/(3.7\times10^{-4})^2 \approx 7.5\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}}\). Each LiDAR point-to-plane correspondence, whitened by lidar_sigma, carries \(1/\sigma^2\) along its plane's normal. Set the two against each other, for correspondences whose normals all lie along one axis:

lidar_sigmainformation per correspondencecorrespondences to match the IMU on one axis
0.05 m400 m⁻²about 18,700
0.02 m2,500 m⁻²about 3,000

Treat these as orders of magnitude, not exact budgets. Real normals spread over three axes, which roughly triples the counts, and glass-lio inflates the IMU factor by the previous state's own uncertainty, which lowers its information. But the ratio between the rows is exact: \((0.05/0.02)^2 = 6.25\). Changing that one number hands the LiDAR six times more say in every argument.

This is not hypothetical. With lidar_sigma at 0.05 m, when the Livox's real point-to-plane noise is about 2 cm, glass-lio's tight coupling let the IMU win arguments it should have lost. The point-to-plane residual has no velocity columns, so position is the only channel that disciplines velocity; with position under-trusted, the IMU over-integrated velocity to about 42 m/s and the trajectory drifted to 1,339 m. Setting lidar_sigma to the sensor's actual 0.02 m brought it to 429 m, level with the loosely coupled baseline, with velocities back near 7 m/s.doc/testing.md, §12.5, “The last drift — an under-trusted LiDAR”, and the table under it.

The lesson in one line

In a fused solve, a noise parameter is not a tuning knob for one sensor; it is a vote on which sensor is right. Set it to the physical truth, measured, and let the covariances argue. When the output looks wrong, check the votes before touching the model.

8 · What this covariance does not contain

Section 3 already warned that \(\Sigma_{ij}\) is conditioned on the integration biases. It is worth listing everything it leaves out, because each omission is handled somewhere else, and forgetting where is how covariances become over-confident.

  • Bias uncertainty. Handled by the bias states and their random-walk prior, not by \(\Sigma_{ij}\). The bias Jacobians of lesson 4 are how bias uncertainty reaches the residual.
  • Uncertainty in the starting state \(\mathbf{x}_i\). Handled in glass-lio by the inflation \(\mathbf{J}_i\mathbf{P}_i\mathbf{J}_i^\top\) from section 1, or in a window by estimating \(\mathbf{x}_i\) jointly. Leaving it out is exactly the “infinitely certain \(\mathbf{x}_i\)” failure lesson 3 ended on.
  • Non-white noise. Vibration from motors or wheels is correlated in time, not white. The recursion assumes independence between samples, so a vibrating platform needs larger effective densities than the datasheet lists, measured on the platform.
  • Discretisation error. Lesson 2's “hold everything constant” error is systematic, not random, so no covariance models it. At 200 Hz it is small enough not to matter; on a slow IMU it would silently bias the estimate while the covariance claimed confidence.
Try it: a platform vibrates so its accelerometer noise is correlated over about 10 samples. Should you raise or lower σa?

Raise it. Correlated noise does not average down as fast as white noise: ten correlated samples carry roughly the information of one. Treating them as independent would claim about three times (\(\sqrt{10}\)) too small a standard deviation for integrated quantities. Inflating the density is the standard, honest fix when you cannot model the correlation.

9 · Practice

From memory, no scrolling. The last question comes from lesson 4.

Why can \(\Sigma_{ij}\) be computed by linear propagation at all?

To first order, Forster's eqs. (42)–(43) write the preintegrated noise as a linear combination of the raw IMU noise, so its covariance follows by linear propagation. The datasheet gives \(\Sigma_{\eta}\), the raw noise, not \(\Sigma_{ij}\).

What is \(\Sigma_{ii}\), the covariance at the very start of the interval?

An empty interval has measured nothing, so its delta carries no uncertainty yet. Forster starts from \(\Sigma_{ii} = \mathbf{0}_{9\times 9}\), and glass-core's cov_ starts at zero.

In the velocity row of \(\mathbf{A}_k\), where does the \(-\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}\,\mathbf{a}_k^{\wedge}\,\Delta t\) block come from?

A small rotation error \(\delta\boldsymbol{\phi}\) points the measured specific force slightly the wrong way. To first order that's \(-\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}\,\mathbf{a}_k^{\wedge}\,\delta\boldsymbol{\phi}\), integrated over \(\Delta t\). Bias uncertainty isn't part of this 9×9 covariance at all.

For scalar velocity driven only by independent white accelerometer noise, \(\operatorname{Var}(v)=\sigma_a^2T\). Double \(T\): what happens to its inverse-variance weight?

In this specified scalar model, \(1/(\sigma_a^2(2T))\) is half the original weight. The full coupled 9×9 propagation does not imply that every direction loses information monotonically.

From lesson 4. In eq. (44), which delta's bias correction is not a plain addition?

Rotations don't add: \(\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}\,\mathrm{Exp}(\mathbf{J}^{R}_{g}\,\delta\mathbf{b}^{g})\), applied on the right. Velocity and position live in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) and just add.

You rerun the same 0.1 s interval with the IMU at 400 Hz instead of 200 Hz. With the discrete noise set correctly, the final \(\Sigma_{ij}\)…

A density describes physical noise per unit bandwidth, so the uncertainty after 0.1 s cannot depend on the sampling rate. If yours changes with the rate, you probably multiplied by \(\Delta t\) instead of dividing.

Changing lidar_sigma from 0.05 m to 0.02 m multiplies the LiDAR's information in the solve by about…

Information is \(1/\sigma^2\), so \((0.05/0.02)^2 = 6.25\). A global scale cancels only when one sensor is alone in the solve; once the IMU shares the normal equations, it decides who wins.

Motor vibration makes a platform's accelerometer noise correlated across samples. The honest fix, if you cannot model the correlation, is to…

The recursion assumes independent samples. Correlated noise averages down more slowly, so the real uncertainty is larger than the model says. Inflating the density fixes the physics; raising a weight above 1 makes the IMU more confident, the wrong direction entirely.

From memory: write the covariance recursion, with its starting value and the discrete IMU noise covariance. Then say in one sentence what its inverse is used for. Stretch: write \(\mathbf{B}_k\).

$$ \begin{aligned} \Sigma_{i,k+1} &= \mathbf{A}_k\,\Sigma_{ik}\,\mathbf{A}_k^{\top} + \mathbf{B}_k\,\Sigma_{\eta}\,\mathbf{B}_k^{\top}\\ \Sigma_{ii} &= \mathbf{0}\\ \Sigma_{\eta} &= \frac{1}{\Delta t}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_g^{2}\mathbf{I} & \mathbf{0}\\ \mathbf{0} & \sigma_a^{2}\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} $$
  • \(\mathbf{A}\Sigma\mathbf{A}^{\top}\): sandwiched, not \(\mathbf{A}\Sigma\)
  • Plus \(\mathbf{B}\Sigma_{\eta}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\), fresh noise every sample
  • Starts from zero
  • \(\sigma^{2}/\Delta t\): divided by the step, not multiplied
  • Its inverse is the weight on the IMU residual
